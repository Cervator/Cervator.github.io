## What will September look like?

At our May budget meeting, the principal of Redwood stood up and kept circling back to one question: *what will September look like?*

I haven't been able to shake it. Because we now know part of the answer. West Orange is facing a budget gap somewhere around $15 million, and the plan that passed outsources more than 200 of our paraprofessionals to a staffing agency. On paper, that looks like a saving. In a classroom, it looks like something else entirely.

A paraprofessional who has worked with a child for years is not a line item. My own oldest has had the same para for nearly a third of his life. That person knows his triggers, his progress, the small signals nobody wrote down. When an agency takes over and the turnover starts — and with these agencies it always starts — that knowledge is simply wiped. You can't undo that with next year's budget.

I'll be honest with you, because I think we're past the point of pretending. I considered despair over this. I genuinely thought about giving up. But despair is a luxury. The kids need us, and right now we're failing them.

## The trap we walked into

Here's the part that took me a while to see. This isn't really a story about one bad budget. It's a story about a slow trade we made, decade by decade, without quite noticing.

Think about how school lunch worked a generation ago. Food was cooked in the building by people who lived down the street. Today, in a lot of districts, that whole operation belongs to a corporate catering company. We were promised convenience and savings. What we actually got was a middleman who pockets a margin, squeezes the workers' pay, and hands the district a bigger bill for worse food.

Now watch the exact same move happen with our paraprofessionals. We keep trading local control for convenience, and the convenience keeps turning into an expensive trap. The budget isn't broken because anyone in particular is foolish. It's broken because there are more hands in the pie than there used to be, each taking a little on the way through.

And I want to be fair about something, because it matters: the people on the board are not villains. I don't doubt for a second that they care about our kids. I think the problem is the format — the weight of "the numbers," the liability, the sheer lack of time. Nobody in that room has the hours to reinvent how a town takes care of itself. Which is exactly the gap I think we can fill.

## The hidden power sitting in our neighborhoods

At the same moment our schools are running out of money, something quietly hopeful is happening in our homes.

A lot of us — especially anyone working in or around technology — are finding that the newest tools let us get our work done noticeably faster than we used to. For the first time in a long while, a real pool of capable, caring people has a little bit of spare time and energy during the week.

Right now that time is scattered and invisible. You might have a free hour on Tuesday morning. Your neighbor has two hours on Thursday. We've simply never had a good way to gather up those fragments and point them at something that matters. The usual answer to a budget crisis is to wait — for the state, for a grant, for some big policy fix that may never come. I don't want to wait. I think we can start now, in fractions, with the time we already have.

## An invisible village

This is not me asking exhausted parents to join another committee or download another confusing app. We all have signup-sheet fatigue. I have it too.

Picture instead a quiet bit of coordination running in the background — connecting people who have a little to give with the specific things our community needs, and asking almost nothing of anyone. I keep calling this a **community exoskeleton**: a support structure built around the schools and the leagues, holding some weight for a while so the institution can stand. The key word is *around*. Neighbors helping neighbors — not another agency.

To the person on the receiving end, it shouldn't feel like technology at all. It should feel like a friendly text from the most organized person you know: *"Hey, the soccer league needs a hand moving cones to Field 3 tomorrow morning — and you're already headed there at 8:45. Can you grab them?"*

## Where we begin: a Saturday at the field

We don't start by fixing the school district. We start where the stakes are low and the burnout is high — our youth sports leagues, our scout troops, the messy beautiful chaos of a Saturday morning.

Every sports parent knows the scramble. Who's bringing whom. Who has the cones. Who signed up for snacks and forgot. Whose cleats just got outgrown for the third season running. These are tiny problems, but they eat real hours and real money. So we take them one at a time:

- A **cleat exchange** — barely more than a group chat and a simple list. Outgrown cleats and shin guards go to the family that needs the next size up, instead of getting tossed or shipped off in a donation bag to be sorted three states away. The value stays right here, between neighbors.
- A **carpool and equipment mesh** that quietly sorts out who's driving and who's hauling gear, so no single overwhelmed parent is texting fifteen people at 9pm.
- **Junior coaches** — high-schoolers coming back to coach the same little-kid sport they grew up playing. It's one of the most natural mentoring relationships a town has, a visible cycle of kids helping kids, and it's the warmest thing on this whole list.

None of that requires anyone's permission. And when a tired parent suddenly finds themselves with a free Saturday morning they didn't expect — that's the moment something clicks. You didn't just save them two hours. You showed them that a neighborhood can still take care of its own.

## The fine print

I'd be doing you a disservice if I made this sound like magic, so here's the part nobody puts on a flyer, in plain terms.

This only works if it's **additive and temporary**. The second anyone uses "the volunteers will handle it" as an excuse to underpay a teacher or a para, the whole thing has curdled into a new kind of exploitation — and I will be the first to say so. The exoskeleton is scaffolding. Scaffolding is supposed to come down once the building can stand on its own. It is never a reason to stop properly funding the people who do this work.

It is also **not** about replacing anyone's job. The goal is to claw back the margin that corporate vendors are skimming — the catering company, the staffing agency — and keep it here, for our own people. Our union employees should be partners in this, not targets of it. And no one person should carry it; I can't, and I shouldn't, and neither should the five people on the board. That's rather the whole point.

## What I actually believe

You hear a lot these days about hidden powers pulling the strings. I don't really buy it. I still believe in a community's ability to do good out in the open. So instead of waiting for a rescue that isn't coming, I'd rather we just start — quietly, locally, one soccer field and one school lunch and one returned Saturday at a time — and let the relief of it speak for itself. Once people feel what a self-reliant village actually feels like, the trust builds, and then we can look at the bigger, scarier things together.
